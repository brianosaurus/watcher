Fail Fast, Fail Often in the Age of AI

2026-11-04

Hypothesis-driven innovation vs. build-first, ask-later.

What “fail fast” means, and where it came from

“Fail fast” is the idea that when something is going to go wrong, you want to find out immediately and cheaply, not late and expensively. It started as an engineering principle, and later became a business slogan.

The engineering version is older. In 1985, Jim Gray, writing about fault tolerance at Tandem Computers in Why Do Computers Stop and What Can Be Done About It?, described “fail-fast” modules: components that either work correctly or stop the moment they detect a fault, instead of limping along and corrupting everything downstream. Jim Shore brought the phrase to everyday programmers in his 2004 IEEE Software article “Fail Fast,” arguing that code should raise an error at the point of the problem, not several layers away where it is hard to trace.

The business version took off in the 2000s and 2010s, as Silicon Valley absorbed the lessons of cheap, rapid experimentation. Steve Blank's customer development and Eric Ries's The Lean Startup (2011) made the argument that a startup's job is validated learning: run the cheapest experiment that could prove you wrong, and change course quickly. Facebook's “move fast and break things” was the same instinct, in cruder form. “Fail fast, fail often” is the bumper-sticker version of all of it.

Both versions rest on the same condition: a failure only helps if you can see it and learn from it. Keep that in mind for what follows.

Why this matters now

Before AI coding tools, building was expensive. A new product or feature meant weeks of engineering time, real money, and a team betting its quarter on the outcome. That cost was a forcing function. It made us stop and ask the uncomfortable questions before committing:

  • What do we believe is true?
  • What would have to be true for this to work?
  • What's the cheapest way to find out if we're wrong?

So we ran the smoke test, did the customer interviews, faked the button and counted clicks. We proved (or killed) the hypothesis first, then built.

Now building is nearly free

An AI agent can spin up a working prototype before lunch. Somewhere along the way, a lot of us quietly dropped the fundamentals. The new loop is: build it, ship it, see what happens. “We'll learn from the data.”

The problem is that you can't learn from data when you never wrote down what you expected. Without a hypothesis, every result looks like a signal, and nothing ever clearly fails. We end up with a graveyard of half-used features, each one “kind of promising,” none of them teaching us anything.

That is the catch in “fail fast, fail often.” Failing only counts if you can tell that you failed. Failing fast is cheap now. Failing legibly is still on you.

Thinking is the scarce resource

Cheap building didn't make thinking optional. It made thinking the only scarce resource left. The cost of a bad idea used to be engineering time. Now it's focus, maintenance, user confusion, and a product nobody can explain.

Before the next build

  1. Write the hypothesis in one sentence: “We believe [user] will [behavior] because [reason].”
  2. Define what success and failure look like before you ship.
  3. Pick a kill date.

Let the agents build fast, and fail fast and often. We still have to decide what's worth building, and to notice when the answer was no.
